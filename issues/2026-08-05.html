Google DeepMind AI Safety Researcher Who Just RESIGNED Says P(Doom) is 25% — Debate with Alex Turner

Alex Turner — alignment PhD, CHAI postdoc under Stuart Russell, co-author of Shard Theory and the steering-vector work behind Anthropic's Golden Gate Claude — resigned from Google DeepMind over its Pentagon contract, which he says carries no binding restriction against autonomous weapons or mass surveillance. He argues Google broke the founding promise it made when it acquired DeepMind, and that the people best placed to slow things down are precisely the ones now leaving. He still puts P(doom) at 25%, which makes this an actual debate rather than an echo chamber.

80,000 Hours PodcastAug 04, 2026

What the hell happened with AGI timelines in 2026? — Rob Wiblin

Six months ago Rob Wiblin recorded an explainer on why AI researchers had pushed their AGI timelines out; by the day he hit publish the consensus had flipped back again. He walks through what genuinely changed — models now completing software engineering tasks that take human professionals a full day, improving faster than the benchmarks can track; Karpathy reversing from "agents are slop" to "alien tools" in two months; Anthropic revenue compounding at an annualised 8,400%. The single most useful summary of where serious timeline estimates now sit and why they moved.

The A.I. Revolt Is Here

Data centers have managed what almost nothing else could — uniting Republicans and Democrats in opposition, with an overwhelming majority of Americans saying they would fight one near where they live. Training Data (Sequoia)Aug 04, 2026

Chai Discovery's Bitter Lesson: Drug Design Is Another Scaling Problem

Chai Discovery co-founders Josh Meier and Matt McPartlon make the case that drug design obeys the bitter lesson: scale the data, models and compute, and the model learns what a hand-built pipeline never could. The concrete result is Chai-2 pushing de novo antibody design from a sub-0.1% hit rate to 16% — turning a needle-in-a-haystack search into something closer to cutting a key to fit a lock. Meier's counterintuitive claim that biology is more verifiable than code is the part worth arguing with.
