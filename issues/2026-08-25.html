Machine Learning Street TalkAug 22, 2026

Stealing Reasoning Traces from Proprietary LLM APIs — Ilia Shumailov & Alexander Panfilov

Ilia Shumailov (ex-Google DeepMind, Cambridge PhD under Ross Anderson) and Alexander Panfilov (ELLIS Tübingen) walk Tim Scarfe through their paper showing that the encrypted reasoning blobs providers hand back so a conversation can be resumed or forked can be replayed across users and sibling models — a smaller model asks the provider to decrypt the trace, then restates the hidden chain of thought in plain text. The fallout runs from leaked private data to a broadly reusable jailbreak and poisoned agent traces, and they are careful to separate the demonstrated attack from ordinary benign distillation. If you run agents against frontier APIs, this is the clearest account yet of why labs guard chain of thought so fiercely — and what breaks when that guard fails.

Must-readA concrete, disclosed attack on the exact hidden-reasoning layer that chain-of-thought monitoring depends on.

Eye on AIAug 24, 2026

95% of AI Agent Projects Fail to Reach Production. Here's Why | Manoj Saxena, TrustWise

Manoj Saxena, the executive who commercialised IBM Watson, tells Craig Smith that building an agent takes hours while shipping one to production takes six or seven months — and that the bottleneck has nothing to do with model quality. His thesis is that intelligence without control is not deployable: enterprises lack runtime infrastructure that can evaluate every tool call and output in milliseconds against the alignment and policy stack governing what the agent is actually allowed to do. He describes TrustWise's control tower doing exactly that across vendors and frameworks in 10–300ms, layered from UN human-rights frameworks down to individual customer SLAs.

Must-readThe best diagnosis going of why agent pilots stall — and it is a governance-plumbing problem, not a model problem.

Odd LotsAug 24, 2026

The Nigerian Industrial Behemoth That Could Reshape the African Economy

Joe Studwell — whose How Asia Works became the standard text on why some countries industrialise and others do not — returns with How Africa Works and a decade of reporting behind it. The hook is Dangote Refinery securing a $1bn underwriting for what will be Africa's largest-ever IPO, but the argument is broader: African states have repeatedly failed to copy the export-manufacturing industrial policy that worked in Asia, which is why resource access never converted into development. Studwell is nonetheless optimistic that the preconditions are finally taking root, with continental growth around 4% in 2026.

Must-readStudwell's development framework applied to the one large economy still genuinely un-priced by global investors.

Training DataAug 18, 2026

Rich Sutton and Khurram Javed: Why AI Models Stop Learning, and How to Start It Again

Rich Sutton — father of reinforcement learning and author of The Bitter Lesson — has cofounded Oak Lab with his former student to build agents that learn continuously from their own experience rather than from us. He calls synthetic data "a big mistake", says catastrophic forgetting is "totally curable" via continual backprop, and rates LLMs a genuine but partial breakthrough worth roughly a quarter of intelligence. Khurram Javed adds the strategic point: the frontier labs sit in a local minimum where switching paradigms gets worse before it gets better, so they cannot follow.

Must-readThe most credible dissent from the scaling consensus, from the person who wrote the essay the scalers quote.

All-In PodcastAug 24, 2026

Michael Kratsios: Trump's Science Agenda, Anti-Science Claims, Fauci's Damage, DEI & China

The White House science chief takes the besties through the administration's research agenda: the Nature poll calling it anti-science, $8bn of cancelled DEI grants, the pulling of RCP 8.5 from climate modelling, and whether $47bn a year at NIH has bought stagnation given a median researcher age of 71. The forward half is the interesting part — Genesis Mission, quantum by 2028, fusion by 2035, crewed lunar return in 2028. The China segment lands the hardest number: research spend from $33bn to $670bn, and seven in ten US STEM PhDs now going to non-Americans.

a16z PodcastAug 24, 2026

Why Medical AI Needs a Referee | Protege's Engy Ziedan

Engy Ziedan, cofounder and chief scientific officer of Protege, argues medical AI has a measurement problem: acing a static benchmark says nothing about whether a model is safe inside a real clinical workflow. She explains how the same model ranks differently depending on prompting and test design, how contaminated training data quietly invalidates benchmarks, and where subtle bias and misalignment hide. Her conclusion — that personalised, fast-changing models need continuous monitoring rather than periodic certification — is the same runtime-evals argument Saxena makes for enterprise agents, arriving from healthcare.

Worth a lookIndependent evaluation as a business: the "who audits the model" gap becomes a market.

DecoderAug 20, 2026

Welcome to the AI crisis in math

Nilay Patel talks to Verge AI reporter Robert Hart, who interviewed working mathematicians about what it means that frontier models went from hopeless to formidable at mathematics in about two years. The reporting separates hype from substance across the recent claims — an unreleased Anthropic model making progress on the Riemann hypothesis, Erdős problems falling, OpenAI's heavily-marketed Astra — and finds genuine advances tangled up with heavy overselling. Useful as a template for how a knowledge profession actually reacts when the machine gets good at the core skill.

Worth a lookA working profession's existential crisis, reported rather than speculated.

The AI Model Tier List

NLW uses a viral tier list as a way into the fact that naming a single "best" model no longer makes sense — cost and latency now weigh as heavily as raw intelligence, and serious buyers are assembling stacks that mix premium and open models per task. The Real Future of AI and Work

Drawing on Every's new Thesis Statements project, NLW pushes past the job-losses headline to ask what actually changes when intelligence is cheap: what individuals spend their day doing, how firms organise, which skills gain value and which quietly stop mattering. Luke Burgis on Mimetic Desire, Catholicism, and the Age of Social Contagion

Luke Burgis, who made René Girard legible to general readers with Wanting, returns with The One and the Ninety-Nine on self and crowd in an age of social contagion. #430 How to Write Ads That Sell

David Senra works through Claude Hopkins' autobiography My Life in Advertising and Scientific Advertising — the book Ogilvy said nobody should work in advertising before reading seven times, and which Hopkins' own agency founder locked in a safe for twenty years. The through-line is measurement: Hopkins invented testing, coupons and tracked response, and insisted advertising is a discipline with answers rather than a matter of taste. Dense with quotes on work rate and the compounding value of knowing one thing better than anyone.

The Bankruptcy That's Ruining Dinner

Blue Apron customers are getting cancelled orders, missing ingredients and half-complete recipes, and the company has publicly conceded that "recent orders have fallen short". WSJ's Sarah Nassauer traces how a bankruptcy works through to the operational layer — what happens to supplier terms, working capital and fulfilment when a consumer subscription business restructures. A short, tidy case study in the gap between a restructuring on paper and a business that still has to ship perishable goods every week.
